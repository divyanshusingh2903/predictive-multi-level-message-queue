\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

The theory of scheduling by size begins with the observation that shortest remaining processing time (SRPT) minimises
mean response time \citep{schrage1968}, and much of what followed was an effort to make that result usable. SOAP
gives a single analysis for a broad class of age-based policies that includes priority classes, SRPT and Gittins-style
indices \citep{scully2018soap}. The standing objection to size-based scheduling is that it penalises long jobs, but for
the heavy-tailed sizes seen in practice analysis finds the penalty to be much smaller than is commonly believed \citep{bansal2001}, and the
different senses in which a policy can be unfair have been classified formally \citep{wierman2003}. Systems work bears
this out: serving small web requests first improved response times with little loss to large ones
\citep{harchol2003}, and across several servers, sending jobs of similar size to the same host avoids short jobs queueing
behind long ones \citep{harchol1999sita}. Size-aware sharding applies the same reasoning to key-value stores for the
same reason \citep{didona2019minos}. \sys{} differs from these in that it is a single non-preemptive queue shared by many
workers. It can only reorder what has not started, so it inherits the penalty on long jobs without the ability to
preempt that some of these systems rely on, and our results show that cost directly.

Those results assume the scheduler knows each job's size. A second body of work asks what to do when it does not.
If only the distribution of sizes is known, the Gittins index minimises mean response time \citep{gittins1979} and
stays close to optimal on many servers \citep{scully2020gittins}; if nothing is known, foreground--background
scheduling favours jobs that have received the least service \citep{nuyens2008}. Tiresias combines the two to schedule
GPU training jobs whose durations nobody can predict \citep{gu2019tiresias}. These policies learn from a running
job's age, which a broker cannot observe, since a handler is opaque code that it cannot preempt. What the broker does
see is the outcome of earlier jobs of the same kind, and that is the signal we use.

Between the two extremes lies scheduling with estimated sizes. The question of how inaccurate estimates affect
size-based policies was first studied by \citet{lu2004}. \citet{dellamico2014} later showed that such policies tolerate
substantial error unless the size distribution is highly skewed, and that the damage is asymmetric: over-estimating a
job delays that job, whereas under-estimating one can let a long job block many others. Theory has since caught up.
\citet{mitzenmacher2019} analyses queues that schedule by predicted service times and introduces the price of
misprediction, and \citet{scully2022uniform} give a variant of SRPT whose performance relative to SRPT approaches one as
estimation error shrinks. The broader programme of algorithms with predictions
\citep{mitzenmacher2022cacm}, now being applied to queues and to LLM serving \citep{mitzenmacher2025queueing}, takes
the prediction as given. Our concern is complementary and more practical: how a broker obtains a usable prediction
without any configuration, and where that prediction breaks.

Several systems have taken exactly that step in other settings. HFSP brings size-based scheduling with aging to Hadoop
and estimates job sizes online while jobs run, showing that the scheduler tolerates the resulting errors
\citep{pastorelli2013}. Supercomputer schedulers face user-supplied runtime estimates that are notoriously poor;
replacing them with the average of a user's last two runtimes improved backfilling \citep{tsafrir2007}, and
machine-learned predictors with purpose-built cost functions improved it further \citep{gaussier2015}. At the other
end of the scale, Decima learns cluster scheduling policies end to end with reinforcement learning
\citep{mao2019decima}. What these systems share with ours is the use of history. What differs is the layer and the
identity of the thing being predicted. They schedule long, batch-like jobs that already carry an identity, such as a
user or a job template, inside a cluster manager. We apply the per-history idea to a general queue of short
jobs, in which the identity must be inferred from the producer and an optional job-type header, and we measure it on
traces where identities recur (serverless functions) and where they do not (two HPC logs).

Serverless platforms face the same mix of short and long work, and recent systems attack it closer to the metal.
Hermod studies scheduling policies for serverless functions and finds that common choices such as late binding and
random load balancing are sub-optimal for typical duration distributions \citep{kaffes2022hermod}. SFS and ALPS schedule
functions inside a host's operating system to approximate SRTF and SRPT, with ALPS learning priorities from recent
workload behaviour \citep{fu2022sfs,fu2024alps}, and Shinjuku reaches microsecond-scale tail latency with very fast
preemption \citep{kaffes2019shinjuku}. These all rely on controlling the processor or placing work across hosts. \sys{}
works one layer up, on the order in which a broker hands messages to workers, and needs neither preemption nor control
over the machine, which is also why it applies to ordinary job queues rather than only to function platforms.

At that layer the brokers people actually run expose no learning. The priorities in Azure's pattern, Meta's queue and
RabbitMQ are set by producers \citep{azurepriority,foqs2021,rabbitmqpriority}; RabbitMQ's priorities only reorder
messages that have not yet been delivered, so they depend on a small consumer prefetch, and its quorum queues use strict
priority, which can starve low-priority messages \citep{rabbitmqpriority}. SQS has no priority \citep{sqstypes}, and
Kafka orders a partition by offset \citep{kreps2011kafka}. We are not aware of a general-purpose broker that learns
message cost and uses it for routing, though our search was not exhaustive. To show the idea is not tied to our
broker, our RabbitMQ experiments run \sys{}'s predictor as an external sidecar that sets those producer-side priorities.

Finally, our evaluation draws on public workloads: the Azure Functions 2021 invocation trace
\citep{zhang2021harvest}, whose earlier characterisation is \citet{shahrad2020}, the Huawei Cloud 2025 request traces
\citep{joosen2024}, and two logs from the Parallel Workloads Archive \citep{feitelson2014}. Before settling on simple
statistics for the predictor we compared eight online learners, including River models \citep{montiel2021}, offline.
None passed every frozen quality gate, and the statistics then passed the scheduling gate, a result we return to in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

Taken together, \sys{} takes the size-based scheduling result that is well established when sizes are known, replaces
the assumption with a per-key estimate learned from acknowledgements, puts the whole mechanism in the broker, and keeps
aging to bound the damage of bad estimates. The policy itself is not new. What this paper adds is the
zero-configuration loop, and evidence about when it helps (production-style job queues, and serverless traces under an
overload-aware aging design) and when it does not.
