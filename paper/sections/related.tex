\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

We organise prior work by the assumption it makes about job sizes, which is the axis on which \sys{} differs.

\subsection{Scheduling when sizes are known}
Shortest remaining processing time (SRPT) minimises mean response time \citep{schrage1968}. The SOAP framework gives a
single analysis for a broad class of age-based policies, including priority classes, SRPT and Gittins-style indices
\citep{scully2018soap}. The main objection to size-based policies is that they penalise long jobs. Analyses of SRPT
find that, for realistic heavy-tailed sizes, large jobs are hurt much less than feared \citep{bansal2001}, and
unfairness has been classified formally \citep{wierman2003}. In practice, serving small web requests first improved
response times with little loss to large ones \citep{harchol2003}. For multi-server systems, size-interval task
assignment sends jobs of similar size to the same host \citep{harchol1999sita}, and size-aware sharding applies the same
idea to key-value stores to avoid head-of-line blocking \citep{didona2019minos}. \sys{} sits at a single queue shared by
many workers and is non-preemptive; it can only reorder what has not started, and so inherits the long-job penalty
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Scheduling when sizes are unknown}
When sizes are unknown but their distribution is known, the Gittins index minimises mean response time
\citep{gittins1979}, and it remains near-optimal on multiple servers \citep{scully2020gittins}. With no information,
foreground--background (least attained service) favours young jobs \citep{nuyens2008}. Tiresias uses a discretised
Gittins index, falling back to least attained service, to schedule GPU training jobs whose durations are unpredictable
\citep{gu2019tiresias}. These policies learn from the age of a running job. \sys{} cannot: a queue decides before the
job starts, and handlers cannot be preempted, so it learns from the \emph{history of the same kind of job} instead.

\subsection{Scheduling with estimated or predicted sizes}
Inaccurate size information has been studied since \citet{lu2004}. \citet{dellamico2014} show that size-based policies
tolerate error unless the size distribution is highly skewed, and that under-estimation causes most of the harm:
over-estimation delays one job, under-estimation can let a long job block many. \citet{mitzenmacher2019} analyses queues
that use predicted service times and introduces the price of misprediction; \citet{scully2022uniform} give a variant
of SRPT whose approximation ratio tends to one as estimation error tends to zero, uniformly. The broader programme of
algorithms with predictions \citep{mitzenmacher2022cacm}, and its application to queues and LLM serving
\citep{mitzenmacher2025queueing}, takes a prediction as given. We take a complementary, systems view: how a broker
obtains a prediction without configuration, from the durations it already observes, and where that prediction fails.

\subsection{Runtime prediction in clusters and supercomputers}
HFSP brings size-based scheduling with aging to Hadoop, estimating job sizes online while jobs run, and shows
tolerance to estimation error \citep{pastorelli2013}. In HPC, user-supplied runtime estimates are
notoriously inaccurate; replacing them with the mean of the user's last two runtimes improved backfilling
\citep{tsafrir2007}, and machine-learned predictors with purpose-built cost functions improved it further \citep{gaussier2015}. Decima learns
cluster schedules with reinforcement learning \citep{mao2019decima}. These systems schedule long, batch-like jobs in
cluster managers with a job identity (a user or a job template). \sys{} applies the per-history idea to a general queue
of short jobs, where the key must be inferred from the producer and a job-type header, and measures it on traces
where keys do recur (serverless functions) and where they do not (two HPC logs).

\subsection{Serverless and microsecond-scale schedulers}
Serverless platforms face the same short-versus-long mix. Hermod studies scheduling policies for serverless functions
and finds that common choices such as late binding and random load balancing are sub-optimal for typical duration
distributions \citep{kaffes2022hermod}. SFS and ALPS schedule functions inside a host's operating system to approximate
SRTF/SRPT, and ALPS learns priorities from recent workload behaviour \citep{fu2022sfs,fu2024alps}. Shinjuku achieves
microsecond-scale tail latency with fast preemption \citep{kaffes2019shinjuku}. These work at the CPU-scheduling or
function-placement layer and rely on preemption or processor control. \sys{} operates one layer up, on the order in
which a broker hands messages to workers, and needs neither.

\subsection{Message brokers}
The common brokers expose no learning. SQS has no priority \citep{sqstypes}; Kafka orders a partition by offset
\citep{kreps2011kafka}; RabbitMQ lets publishers set a priority per message, which works only with a small consumer
prefetch, and its quorum queues use strict priority, which can starve low-priority messages \citep{rabbitmqpriority}. We are not aware of a
general-purpose broker that learns message cost and uses it to route, though our search was not exhaustive. Our
RabbitMQ experiments use \sys{}'s predictor as an external sidecar that sets those priorities, showing the idea is not
tied to our broker.

\subsection{Workloads}
We use the Azure Functions 2021 invocation trace \citep{zhang2021harvest} (the earlier Azure characterisation is
\citep{shahrad2020}), the Huawei Cloud 2025 request traces \citep{joosen2024}, and two logs from the Parallel Workloads
Archive \citep{feitelson2014}. Eight online learners, including River models \citep{montiel2021}, were compared offline
with per-key statistics; none passed every frozen gate, and statistics then passed the scheduling gate
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Positioning}
In sum, \sys{} takes the size-based scheduling result that is well established when sizes are known, replaces the
assumption with a per-key estimate learned from acknowledgements, and places the whole mechanism in the broker, with
aging to bound the damage of bad estimates. What is new here is not the policy but the zero-configuration loop, the
evidence of when it helps (production-style job queues; serverless traces under an overload-aware aging design), and
the evidence of when it does not.
