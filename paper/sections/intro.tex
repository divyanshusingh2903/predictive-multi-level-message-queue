\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Almost every large service is built around queues. A web request that would take too long to finish synchronously becomes a message, and a pool of workers picks it up
later. Cloud vendors document the pattern as a design pattern, because a queue between a task and the service it calls smooths bursts of demand that would otherwise
overload the service \citep{azureleveling}, and serverless platforms use it to connect functions to the rest of the cloud, with a managed fleet of pollers that read from a queue and invoke a
function for each batch of messages \citep{lambdasqs}. Inside large companies it runs at a scale that is hard to picture: Meta describes an internal queueing service that processes close to a trillion items a day, on behalf of
notifications, integrity checks, video encoding and the many other tasks that microservices hand to one another asynchronously \citep{foqs2021}. Queues also carry the machinery of reliability, namely retries,
separate retry queues so that one bad message does not block a batch, and dead-letter queues for the messages that never succeed \citep{uberdlq}. When the work behind a service is not what a user is waiting on, it is in a queue.

The trouble is that one queue usually carries very different kinds of work. A notification that takes a few milliseconds shares a queue, and often a pool of workers, with a report export that takes tens of seconds, and when load rises the cheap
job waits behind the expensive one. This is head-of-line blocking, the same mechanism that makes tail latency so hard to control in interactive services \citep{dean2013tail}, and a known concern for microservice applications in which
many such calls compose \citep{gan2019deathstar}. It hurts the mean and the median as well as the tail, because most jobs are the cheap ones.

Practitioners have a standard answer, which is to prioritise. The Azure Architecture Center describes a pattern in which the producer assigns each message a priority and consumers handle higher priorities first, or in which separate queues are kept per priority
\citep{azurepriority}; Meta's queue and RabbitMQ also let producers set a priority on every item \citep{foqs2021,rabbitmqpriority}, whereas SQS offers standard and FIFO queues but no priority \citep{sqstypes} and a Kafka partition is consumed in offset order \citep{kreps2011kafka}. Even where priorities exist, they put the
burden on a person. Someone has to know what each endpoint costs, decide where it belongs, and revisit that decision when code or traffic changes, and getting it wrong is not harmless: in our benchmark a plausible but misconfigured map was slower than doing nothing at all, by \VTwoMisconfVsFifoAbs{} locally and \EOneMisconfVsFifoAbs{} on AWS.

Scheduling theory suggests a route that needs no such person. If a job's processing time is known, serving the shortest first minimises mean response time \citep{schrage1968}, and that idea has been put to work in web servers \citep{harchol2003}, data-processing clusters \citep{pastorelli2013} and
GPU cluster managers \citep{gu2019tiresias}. All of these assume that the scheduler knows a job's size or can watch the job run. A message broker can do neither. The handler is arbitrary code behind a network call, it cannot be preempted, and nobody declared its cost. This gap is what the paper addresses.

What a broker can do is remember. It delivers every message and receives the acknowledgement, so it can measure how long each kind of message actually took and use that history to predict the next one. We build this into \sys{}. For each message key, the producer optionally joined to a job-type header, the broker keeps a decaying histogram of
handler durations reported on acknowledgement, looks up the key's expected duration when a message arrives, and places the message in one of a few priority levels. A key with too little, too old or too variable a history goes to a default level instead. Nothing is trained and the operator configures no per-endpoint priorities; the predictor's own parameters are fixed defaults (\Cref{tab:params}). Because estimates will be wrong, and under-estimating a long job does far more damage
than over-estimating a short one \citep{dellamico2014}, \sys{} keeps aging in place, so a long job that was misjudged, or simply waited a long time, still moves forward.

We evaluate the design the way one should evaluate a claim that could easily be wrong: with experiments whose configurations and pass criteria were frozen before the registered runs, with an oracle as a reference, and with the failures reported next to the successes. On a production-style job queue the approach reduced mean latency by \EOneMeanVsFifoAbs{} against FIFO on AWS, beat hand-tuned priorities, and, unlike a
misconfigured map, did not make things worse. Run as a sidecar next to RabbitMQ, the same predictor cut mean latency by \EThreeSidecarAbs{}, so the idea does not depend on our broker. It is not the only way to the gain: a queue per job type with no prediction did better on mean latency in the same benchmark, and on one trace a static map from the same history did as well, so what the predictor offers is the absence of per-type configuration. It also has clear limits, which the traces make visible: when backlogs last for hours the broker's default aging appears to erase the predicted order, which switching aging off restored on the production flow and on Huawei (we did not test it on Azure); when keys do not recur within the predictor's memory, as in an HPC log whose keys mostly sit idle for longer than that, there is little to learn from; and when long jobs
carry most of the work, favouring short ones can make the overall result worse. Because the broker cannot preempt, it cannot shorten a long job's own service time, and we make no claim that tail latency improves.

This paper makes four contributions. First, it presents a broker design in which per-key duration statistics, learned from the acknowledgements the broker already receives, drive multi-level priority scheduling with no per-endpoint configuration, together with an opt-in worker-time weighting that keeps the order meaningful under sustained overload. Second, it reports a pre-registered evaluation with an oracle reference, hand-tuned and misconfigured
baselines, a queue per job type, a replication on separate AWS hardware, and a comparison with RabbitMQ and Redis, and it measures what the broker's aging costs. Third, it reports results on four public traces from two serverless providers and two HPC logs, two replayed through the real broker and two simulated, with a check of the simulator against the broker, keeping the negative results and explaining them. Fourth, it gives a plain account of the trade-off, including a short queueing explanation of why the mean falls while the tail does not. All evidence is synthetic or trace-driven, and we make no claim of production readiness
(\Cref{sec:limits}).

The rest of the paper follows this argument. \Cref{sec:related} places the work among earlier approaches to scheduling with unknown or estimated sizes, and \Cref{sec:design} describes the broker, its predictor and each optional feature. \Cref{sec:method} explains the pre-registered method, \Cref{sec:results} reports the results, successes and failures alike, and \Cref{sec:limits} states what our evidence does not show.
