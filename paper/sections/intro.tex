\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Almost every large service is built around queues. A web request that would take too long to finish synchronously is
turned into a message, and a pool of workers picks it up later. The pattern has become so standard that the cloud
vendors document it as a design principle: a queue between a task and the service it calls smooths bursts of demand
that would otherwise overload the service \citep{azureleveling}. It is also how serverless platforms connect to the
rest of the cloud, with a managed fleet of pollers that read from a queue and invoke a function for each message
\citep{lambdasqs}. Inside large companies the same idea runs at a scale that is hard to picture; Meta describes an
internal queueing service that processes close to a trillion items a day, with backlogs reaching hundreds of billions,
on behalf of notifications, integrity checks, video encoding and the many other tasks that microservices hand to one
another asynchronously \citep{foqs2021}. Queues also carry the machinery of reliability: retries after failure,
separate retry queues so that one bad message does not block a batch, and dead-letter queues for the ones that never
succeed \citep{uberdlq}. In short, when the work behind a service is not the work a user is waiting on, it is in a queue.

The trouble is that one queue usually carries very different kinds of work. A notification that takes a few
milliseconds shares a queue, and often a pool of workers, with a report export that takes tens of seconds, and when
load rises the cheap job waits behind the expensive one. This is head-of-line blocking, the same mechanism that makes
tail latency so hard to control in interactive services \citep{dean2013tail}, and it is a known weakness of
microservice applications in which many such calls compose \citep{gan2019deathstar}. It hurts the mean and the median
as well as the tail, because most jobs are the cheap ones.

Practitioners have a standard answer, which is to prioritise. The Azure Architecture Center describes a priority-queue
pattern in which the producer assigns each message a priority and consumers handle higher priorities first, or in
which separate queues are kept per priority, sometimes with larger worker pools behind the urgent ones
\citep{azurepriority}. Meta's queue lets producers set a priority on every item \citep{foqs2021}, and RabbitMQ lets
publishers do so too \citep{rabbitmqpriority}. Other services offer no priority at all: Amazon SQS has none
\citep{sqstypes}, and a Kafka partition is consumed in offset order \citep{kreps2011kafka}. Even where priorities
exist, they put the burden on a person. Someone has to know what each endpoint costs, decide where it belongs, and
revisit that decision when code or traffic changes. The same guidance warns that strict priority can delay low-priority
work indefinitely and suggests raising the priority of old messages over time \citep{azurepriority}. Getting the map
wrong is not harmless: in our benchmark a plausible but misconfigured map was slower than doing nothing at all.

Scheduling theory suggests a different route that does not need a person to classify the work. If a job's processing
time is known, serving the shortest first minimises mean response time on one server \citep{schrage1968}, an idea that
underlies web servers that favour small requests \citep{harchol2003}, schedulers for data-processing clusters
\citep{pastorelli2013}, and policies that route jobs of similar size to the same host \citep{harchol1999sita}. The
price is that long jobs wait longer, and whether that is unfair has been argued at length
\citep{bansal2001,wierman2003}. When sizes are not known but their distribution is, the Gittins index takes over
\citep{gittins1979,scully2020gittins}, and with no information at all the foreground--background policy favours the
jobs that have received the least service \citep{nuyens2008}; GPU cluster managers use both \citep{gu2019tiresias}.
All of these assume that the scheduler either knows a job's size or can watch the job run. A message broker can do
neither. The handler is arbitrary code behind a network call, it cannot be preempted, and nobody declared its cost.

What the broker can do is remember. It delivers every message and receives the acknowledgement, so it can measure how
long each kind of message actually took and use that history to predict the next one. This is the idea we explore
with \sys{}. For each message key, the producer optionally joined to a job-type header, the broker keeps a decaying
histogram of handler durations reported on acknowledgement. When a message arrives it looks up the key's expected
duration and places the message in one of a few priority levels. A key with too little history, a stale history or a
wide spread of durations goes to a default level instead. Nothing is trained, and the operator configures no
priorities. The literature also tells us what to guard against: estimates will be wrong, and under-estimating a long
job does far more damage than over-estimating a short one \citep{dellamico2014}. \sys{} therefore keeps aging in
place, so a long job that was misjudged, or simply waited a long time, still moves forward.

We evaluated this design the way one should evaluate a claim that could easily be wrong: with experiments whose
configurations and pass criteria were frozen before the registered runs, with an oracle as a reference, and with the
failures reported alongside the successes. On a production-style job queue the approach reduced mean latency by
\EOneMeanVsFifo{} against FIFO on AWS, beat hand-tuned priorities, and, unlike a misconfigured map, did not make
things worse. Run as a sidecar next to RabbitMQ, the same predictor cut mean latency by \EThreeSidecar{}, so the idea
does not depend on our broker. It also has clear limits, which the traces make visible: when backlogs last for hours the
broker's own aging erases the predicted order; when keys do not recur, as in two HPC logs, there is nothing to learn
from; and when long jobs carry most of the work, favouring short ones can make the overall result worse. Because the
broker cannot preempt, it cannot shorten a long job's own service time, and we make no claim that tail latency
improves.

The rest of the paper follows this argument. Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work among earlier approaches to
scheduling with unknown or estimated sizes, and Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the broker and its predictor. We
explain the pre-registered method in Section~\ref{sec:method}, report the results, successes and failures alike, in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, and state what our evidence does not show in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.
