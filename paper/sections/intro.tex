\section{Introduction}

Background work (e-mail, image resizing, report exports, webhooks) usually shares one queue, and jobs of very
different cost wait behind each other. If service times were known, serving the shortest first would lower mean
latency \citep{schrage1968}. Production queues rarely know them: operators split queues or assign priorities by hand,
per endpoint, and must keep that map correct as code and traffic change. A mistaken map is worse than none; in our
benchmark a plausible misconfiguration was slower than FIFO.

We ask whether a broker can learn the map itself. \sys{} keeps, for each message key (the producer, optionally joined
to a job-type header), a decaying histogram of the handler durations reported on acknowledgement. At ingress it looks
up the key's expected duration and maps it to a priority level using boundaries taken from global quantiles. Cold,
stale or high-variance keys take a default level. The predictor is plain statistics inside the broker; no model is
trained or served.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A broker design with an in-process per-key duration predictor, shadow and predictive routing modes, and an
        opt-in worker-time weighting with pausing aging for sustained overload (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
  \item A pre-registered evaluation with an oracle reference, hand-tuned and misconfigured static baselines, and an
        independent-host AWS replication and comparison with RabbitMQ and Redis (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Trace-driven simulations on four public traces from two serverless providers and two HPC logs, with the
        negative results kept: where the gate failed, and why (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item The scheduling trade-off stated plainly: for non-preemptive queues, shortest-expected-first lowers median and
        mean latency but does not improve P99 when long jobs are common, and costs long jobs.
\end{itemize}
We do not claim a tail-latency improvement, and all evidence is synthetic or trace-driven (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
