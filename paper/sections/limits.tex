\section{Limitations and threats to validity}\label{sec:limits}

The most important limitation is the kind of evidence. Everything in this paper is synthetic or trace-driven, and we
have no production workload. The production-flow scenario was written by us, and the traces, though real, are
simulated or, for Azure and Huawei, replayed through the real broker with handlers that sleep for the recorded duration and so do no work, which removes the contention that real handlers cause: arrivals are reconstructed where a trace does not record them, and the Huawei timestamps are completion times,
so the arrival process we replay is an inference. For Azure, arrival is the end timestamp minus the duration, from timestamps the dataset authors modified, and the 2\% of rows that share an arrival time are ordered as the converted file lists them, which alone moves the weighted arms by up to 2.5\%. The simulator also makes modelling choices that real platforms do not: it queues on 64 workers and scales arrivals to a target load, where a serverless platform would scale out instead. A parallel job on DAS2-fs2 occupies one simulated worker, LPC-EGEE is restricted to single-processor jobs, and the Huawei trace is one region on one day. The experiments ran in one cloud provider, one region and two instance types, which gives a low-latency network and says nothing about a wide-area deployment. The
thirty-minute run has only two repetitions, so its checks are per repetition and carry no intervals. The Azure replay ran once per cell, so it carries no measure of run-to-run noise (Huawei's three repeats agreed to within a few percent, and no cell was unstable), and its load-0.8 gate passes by 0.3 points. Two of the 76 replay cells lost the broker within seconds of starting, one on each pair of hosts, and were re-run once as pre-registered; both retries are valid, but we do not know the cause, and it may be a rare startup defect in the broker. The aging-off replay ran on smaller hosts than the main replay. We did not replay the two research-cluster logs, whose results are simulated. The local production-flow results depend on CPU speed, as the comparison with the original run in \Cref{sec:results} shows, so that run's absolute effect sizes should not be carried to other hardware.

We checked the simulator against the broker twice. The first check is on the production flow, the only workload of ours whose handlers do real computation. The measured production-flow runs, local and AWS, were replayed through the simulator with their own arrivals and handler durations, under criteria frozen before the run (\Cref{tab:insample}; the pre-registration is \texttt{v4-simvalidation-1}). The simulator reproduced the direction of predictive routing's effect in every phase and the order of the policies in all \SimValSeedsGroupOrder{} seeds of both sets, but it failed the bar for the size of the effect: against FIFO it reported a mean-latency reduction of \SimValPredLocalSim{} for predictive routing where the broker achieved \SimValPredLocalReal{} locally, and \SimValPredAWSSim{} against \SimValPredAWSReal{} on AWS, and its gap was \SimValGapMin{} to \SimValGapMax{} points for predictive routing and the oracle against a limit of 10. In relative terms it overstated the reduction by about \SimValOverstatePct{}. Its absolute latencies were only \SimValRatioMin{} to \SimValRatioMax{} of the measured ones. We did not tune the simulator in response. The simulator has no broker overhead, and it replays each message once, so the work of the retried messages of the failing job type is missing. The second check came later, on the traces themselves: the Azure and Huawei traces were replayed through the broker, and the simulator agreed with it to within \SimReplayGapMax{} points in every arm and cell (\Cref{sec:simreplay}). The checks differ in what the workload does. The production flow's handlers compute, and the replayed handlers sleep, so the replay tests the simulator's queueing and cannot test the effect of contention. We therefore read the replayed traces' results as measured and the two research-cluster logs' as simulated, with their directions and the ordering of policies supported, and their sizes uncertain by as much as the production-flow check suggests for work that computes.

Some limitations come from how the design learns. Durations are learned from handlers that succeeded, so a key whose
slow cases fail looks faster than it is. The predictor's parameters (\Cref{tab:params}) were fixed by judgement and varied only afterwards, one at a time and in the simulator (\Cref{sec:sensitivity}); we did not rerun the variants through the broker, so how sensitive the real-broker results are is not measured, and the weighted design was built in response to the Azure result, so that trace is in-sample for it (\Cref{tab:insample}). The budgets of the first experiment were set after seeing earlier offline
data, as \Cref{sec:method} says, so that gate is less independent than the later ones. And the evidence about
learned models is narrow: the offline comparison used a workload whose only feature was a three-valued category, which
leaves a per-key average close to the best possible predictor and little for a richer model to find. We conclude that
statistics were enough for these workloads, not that models never help.

Others come from the scheduler. A non-preemptive queue cannot shorten a long job's own service time, so we make no claim that
all-message tail latency improves; in the production flow it stays within \EOnePNinetyNine{} of FIFO's. The tails of individual classes do move, by about \PerClassCheapPNinetyNineCut{} down for the cheapest classes and by about \PerClassMidPNinetyNineMax{} up for the mid-cost ones (\Cref{fig:perclass}). Where long jobs carry most of the work, favouring short ones can make the overall result worse, as
the DAS2 log shows. The comparison with a queue per job type is on one workload, which is that design's best case, nine job types of nearly fixed cost; we did not test it where types are many or costs vary within a type. The broker is a prototype with in-memory state and no durability, authentication or backpressure, and it
had the lowest throughput of the three brokers we measured. The comparison with RabbitMQ is also bounded by our
configuration: RabbitMQ 3.12 ran with classic priority queues and a prefetch of one, which is how priorities take effect at all, and which limits its
throughput. Kafka, Pulsar, managed queues and durable modes were out of scope, and we did not run Alibaba's GPU traces.

Finally, the code, configurations, pre-registrations and result files are in the repository at
\url{https://github.com/divyanshusingh2903/predictive-multi-level-message-queue}, with the raw per-message data archived
outside git and hashed with SHA-256, so that the published files can be checked against them. The version described here is the release tagged \texttt{v0.2.0-preprint}.
