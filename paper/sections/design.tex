\section{Design}\label{sec:design}

\sys{} is a single-queue message broker. Producers \texttt{Submit} messages over gRPC, consumers \texttt{Pull} them and
then \texttt{Ack} or \texttt{Nack} the outcome, and between the two the broker holds each message in one of a small number
of priority levels (three in all our experiments). Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the path a message takes. On arrival
a thin proxy stamps an identifier, the arrival time and the producer's identity, and hands the message to a single routing
function, \texttt{route\_message()}, which decides the level. Consumers never see the levels: a consumer asks for work and
the broker chooses which message to give it, so no client has to know that priorities exist. When the consumer
acknowledges a message it reports how long its handler took, and that duration flows back to the predictor that decides
where the next message of the same kind goes. The loop between the acknowledgement and the routing function is the whole
idea of the paper; everything else in this section is either the machinery around it or an optional change we made after
an experiment showed a weakness.

Figure~\ref{fig:sequence} follows one message through every exchange, in order. A producer's \texttt{Submit} reaches the proxy, which
adds the identifier, the arrival time and the producer's identity and passes the message to the routing function. The routing
function asks the predictor for the key's level and receives either that level or, when the history is not trustworthy, the
default. It then places the message in the queue. Nothing about the level is visible from there on: a consumer's \texttt{Pull}
returns the message with an attempt token, and the lease that bounds the delivery starts at that moment. The consumer runs
its handler, measures how long it took, and reports that duration with its \texttt{Ack}. The broker checks the token, the lease and the
message's time to live, settles the delivery, and only then tells the predictor the duration, which is why a late or replayed
acknowledgement teaches nothing. The failure paths are summarised under the figure: a \texttt{Nack}, or a lease that expires first, puts the
message back at the tail of its original level until its retries run out, and an expired lease leaves a censored observation in place of a duration.

We built the broker in stages, and the order matters because each stage was shaped by what the previous one measured. The
first stage was an ordinary broker with no prediction at all. It has a fixed number of priority levels, an optional aging
thread that promotes messages that have waited too long, retries on \texttt{Nack}, a dead-letter queue for messages that
exhaust their retries or expire, and per-delivery attempt tokens and leases, so that a crashed consumer's message is
recovered while a stale acknowledgement cannot settle a newer delivery. Delivery is at-least-once for as long as the process
runs, and all state is in memory. Static routing, in which every message gets the same default level, is what this stage
gives and what the broker still does by default. It is also the baseline against which every later feature is measured.

Figure~\ref{fig:states} shows the delivery states that this stage defines. A message is queued until a \texttt{Pull} selects it, and it is then in
flight, owned by one consumer under one attempt token. An acknowledgement within the lease completes it; a \texttt{Nack} or an expired lease
returns it to the tail of its original level while retries remain; and exhausting its retries, or the expiry of its time to live, sends it to the dead-letter
queue. The state machine is the same with or without prediction, which is what lets the predictor be added without changing delivery semantics.

The second stage added prediction. We first planned a separate classifier service fed with features captured at ingress and
with durations persisted by the broker, and we built that plumbing: a bounded, versioned capture of ingress features and a
bounded on-disk feedback log, both optional and neither able to change delivery. We then used it to compare eight online
learners, including River regressors and trees, against simple statistics on delayed feedback. None of the eight passed
every frozen quality criterion, and the workload offered little for a richer model to exploit, so we chose the simplest
option. The predictor is therefore an ordinary lookup, compiled into the broker, with no model to train or serve. Each key
is the producer's identifier, optionally joined to a job-type header that the producer chooses to send; scoping the header by
producer means that a producer can only mislabel its own work and cannot borrow another producer's fast history to jump the
queue. For each key the broker keeps a histogram of handler durations, geometric from one millisecond to ten minutes, whose
older observations decay, and one histogram over every key. A successful, first-time duration reported on an acknowledgement
is added to the message's key. At ingress the broker takes the median of the key's histogram as the expected duration and maps
it to a level using boundaries that come from quantiles of the global histogram, so that the levels adapt to the workload
instead of being set by hand. The lookup costs about a hundred nanoseconds in isolation and about two microseconds inside
the running broker.

Figure~\ref{fig:predictor} lays out the two paths through the predictor. The learning path is the one at the left: only an accepted settlement
contributes, the key's histogram and the global histogram both grow, and the boundaries are recomputed periodically and move only when they
have shifted by more than a small margin, so that a single unusual job cannot reshuffle the levels. The routing path at the right is a short
sequence of questions, each of which can send the message to the default level, and only a message that passes all of them is routed by its estimate.

first boundary and is routed to the highest-priority level, one whose handlers take tens to hundreds of milliseconds falls in the middle, and one that takes seconds goes to the
lowest. The last key is the case the tail check exists for. Ninety-two percent of its jobs are fast, so its median would put it in the top level, but eight percent take thousands of
times longer, and routing it by its median would let those jobs delay everything behind them. Its ninety-ninth percentile is far above sixteen times its median, so it is
routed to the default level instead.

A predictor that routes on its own estimate has to know when not to trust it, and most of this stage's detail is about
that. A key with too few observations, one that has gone unobserved for too long, or one whose tail is far above its median
is sent to the middle level instead. The tail check looks at the ninety-ninth percentile rather than the ninetieth: a test
showed that a key whose jobs take three milliseconds ninety-two percent of the time and three seconds the rest looks
unremarkable at the ninetieth percentile and was routed to a fast level, which is exactly the costly mistake the literature
warns about \citep{dellamico2014}. A delivery that overran its lease yields no duration, so it is recorded as censored, and a
key with many censored deliveries also falls back; dropping them would have made a key whose handlers hang look fast. Memory is
bounded, with a fixed number of keys over sharded locks, and the predictor can run in a shadow mode that records its
predictions while routing statically, which isolates the cost of prediction from its effect. The first experiments
(Section~\ref{sec:results}) passed on a production-style job queue but not on the Azure trace simulation, where no load passed
its gate. Predictive routing therefore stayed an opt-in.

The trace simulation also exposed a defect in the predictor, which we fixed. Staleness was measured from a key's last
\emph{completed} job, so during a backlog lasting hours a key whose jobs were stuck in the queue completed none, and its new
messages were judged stale and sent to the default level. The predictor switched itself off for exactly the keys caught in the
backlog, and in an exploratory run a third to a half of the predictions made during backlogs were stale verdicts, against well
under one percent otherwise. Activity now includes a prediction made while the key was still fresh, so keys that are waiting in
a backlog stay predicted, though a stale key is revived only by new evidence.

The third stage was size-binned keys. A per-key median ignores the fact that some jobs cost what their input costs, and a
measurement on self-generated data with real handlers asked whether a richer model was needed to capture that. It was not.
A per-key median was off by a typical factor of \FSPerKeyFactor{} and placed jobs in the correct level \FSPerKeyTier{} of the
time, while appending the logarithm of the input size to the key brought the factor down to \FSBinnedFactor{} and the accuracy
up to \FSBinnedTier{}, and a gradient-boosted model over every feature did no better at \FSBoostTier{}. So we added the bin to
the key, not a model: an optional log$_2$ size bin, read from the payload length or from a numeric header, with a rule that a
bin that has not yet seen enough jobs borrows its un-binned parent's estimate and never another key's, and with each job
teaching both the bin and its parent. Under load the result was weaker than the measurement suggested. The bins improved the
predictor's accuracy, but mean latency changed by only \SizeBinsMeanCI{} against job-type keys, short of the pre-registered
bar, and one job type whose cost does not follow its size did \SizeBinsInvoiceAbs{} worse. They remain an opt-in.

The fourth stage addressed aging. With the broker's default five-second aging, every message that waits longer than that is
promoted to the top level, so a backlog that lasts minutes or hours collapses all levels into one FIFO and the predicted
order disappears. This was the reason the Azure simulation did not pass, and the oracle did no better. We therefore replaced
strict priority, as an option, with a scheme in which the levels share worker time by weights. Each level keeps a virtual
clock, a pull serves the non-empty level with the smallest clock, and the level is charged the message's expected cost divided
by its weight, so that with weights of 8, 3 and 1 the levels receive about two thirds, a quarter and a twelfth of worker time
while all have work. Figure~\ref{fig:weights} applies the rule to a backlog in which every level always has work. Promotion is then paused into a level that is already behind its share, so that aging cannot refill the
top level during a backlog. The first implementation of the pausing rule was wrong, and we corrected it before
pre-registering the experiment. On the Azure trace it cut mean latency against FIFO by \AgingSimHalfAbs{}, \AgingSimEightAbs{} and
\AgingSimNinetyFiveAbs{} at loads of 0.5, 0.8 and 0.95, and by \AgingSimVsTodayHalfAbs{} to \AgingSimVsTodayNinetyFiveAbs{} against the
default aging, but its gate passed only at load 0.8. On the production flow it cut mean latency by \AgingProdMeanVsPredAbs{}
against predictive routing with default aging, but raised all-message P99 by \AgingProdPNinetyNine{}, over its ten-percent
guardrail, so that gate failed too. The weights and the pausing rule therefore stay opt-in. Later experiments on other
traces and on RabbitMQ (Section~\ref{sec:results}) are the stronger evidence for them.

Alongside these stages, we built the operational pieces that make the predictor safe to run: atomic, versioned snapshots of
predictor state with a rollback copy, so that a restart does not start cold and a corrupt file is refused rather than
trusted; counters for each fallback reason; drift signals, namely rolling agreement between predicted and actual level and a
running average of the log error, which are informational and never change the routing; and a standalone configuration file
with a one-line switch back to static routing. None of these changes the order in which messages are delivered. They are what
let an operator turn prediction on in shadow mode, look at what it would have done, and turn it off again.

Table~\ref{tab:optin} collects the optional features, why each was added, and what the experiments showed. The pattern is
worth stating plainly. Each feature was added in response to a specific weakness, each was tested against a criterion set
before the run, and none of them passed every gate it faced, which is why the broker's defaults are still static priority
with no prediction.

\subsection{The mechanism in equations}\label{sec:equations}

The routing rule is simple enough to state exactly, and stating it removes any doubt about what the experiments measured.
Write $x_{k,1},\dots,x_{k,n_k}$ for the successful handler durations observed so far for key $k$, in the order they were
acknowledged, and let $H_k$ be the key's histogram over a fixed set of geometric duration bins $b$. With a decay factor
$d<1$, each observation's weight shrinks by $d$ every time a newer one arrives,
\begin{equation}
  H_k(b) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n_k} d^{\,n_k-i}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[x_{k,i}\in b\right],
  \qquad
  \hat{x}_k \;=\; Q_{1/2}(H_k),
  \label{eq:histogram}
\end{equation}
where $Q_q(\cdot)$ is the $q$-quantile of a histogram, and the median $\hat{x}_k$ is the key's expected duration. After
$h = \ln(1/2)/\ln d$ new observations an old regime carries half the weight, which is how quickly the predictor forgets (about
138 observations for $d=0.995$). A second histogram $G$ pools every key. With $L$ levels, its quantiles supply the boundaries,
and the level is the number of boundaries that the estimate has reached,
\begin{equation}
  \beta_j \;=\; Q_{j/L}(G),\quad j=1,\dots,L-1,
  \qquad
  \ell(k) \;=\; \bigl|\{\,j : \beta_j \le \hat{x}_k\,\}\bigr|,
  \label{eq:level}
\end{equation}
so that level $0$, the highest priority, holds the shortest expected jobs. The estimate is used only when it is trustworthy.
Otherwise the message takes the default (middle) level $\ell_0$. The key must have at least $n_{\min}$ observations; it must have
been active within the last $\tau$ (thirty minutes); its tail must be close to its median, $Q_{0.99}(H_k)\le\kappa\,Q_{1/2}(H_k)$ with
$\kappa=16$; and the decayed share $c_k$ of its deliveries that overran their lease must not exceed $\gamma=5\%$. Only a message that passes all four is
routed by Equation~\eqref{eq:level}. Time-based decay, eviction and the hysteresis that stops boundaries from jittering are omitted
here for clarity.

The opt-in weights replace strict priority with a share of worker time. Each level $\ell$ has a weight $w_\ell$ and a virtual
clock $v_\ell$, starting at zero. A pull serves the non-empty level with the smallest clock and charges that level the
message's expected cost $\hat{c}$ divided by its weight,
\begin{equation}
  \ell^{\star} \;=\; \arg\min_{\ell\,:\,\text{level }\ell\text{ non-empty}} v_\ell,
  \qquad
  v_{\ell^{\star}} \;\leftarrow\; v_{\ell^{\star}} + \frac{\hat{c}}{w_{\ell^{\star}}},
  \label{eq:clock}
\end{equation}
so that, while every level has work, level $\ell$ receives a fraction $w_\ell/\sum_{j} w_j$ of worker time: two thirds, a
quarter and a twelfth for the weights $(8,3,1)$ that we use. An empty level cannot save credit for later. Pausing aging then skips
the promotion of a message from level $\ell$ to $\ell-1$ for as long as level $\ell-1$ is behind, meaning that it still holds
promoted messages and its oldest message has waited at least the aging threshold, which is what keeps aging from refilling the top
level during a backlog.
