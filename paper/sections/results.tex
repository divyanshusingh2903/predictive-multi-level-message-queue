\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We begin where the design was meant to work, a shared job queue whose job types differ in cost. In the production-flow
benchmark, predictive routing passed its pre-registered gate (Table~\ref{tab:prodflow}). Against FIFO it reduced mean latency
by \VTwoMeanVsFifo{} and the median latency of short jobs by \VTwoShortPFiftyVsFifo{}, while all-message P99 stayed within
\VTwoPNinetyNine{} of FIFO's and no work was lost. It also did better than what an operator would have done. Compared with a
hand-tuned static map it was \VTwoVsTuned{} lower in mean latency, and compared with the plausible but misconfigured map it
was \VTwoVsMisconf{} lower, a map that was itself slower than FIFO. Even without job-type labels, with one key per producer, it
reduced mean latency by \VTwoZeroConfig{}, about half the benefit of labelling. The replication on separate AWS hardware
reproduced every verdict, with a mean reduction of \EOneMeanVsFifo{} and a short-job median reduction of
\EOneShortPFiftyVsFifo{}. The predictor also sat close to its ceiling: the oracle and the predictive arm were statistically
indistinguishable.

The result does not depend on the broker being ours. Run against RabbitMQ and Redis on the same workload
(Table~\ref{tab:brokers}), \sys{}'s predictive arm reduced mean latency by \EThreeVsRabbit{} against RabbitMQ with FIFO and by
\EThreeVsRedis{} against Redis with FIFO, and the gate passed. The margin was smaller than we had predicted, partly
because \sys{}'s own FIFO arm was \EThreeFifoGap{} slower than RabbitMQ's, just outside the pre-registered equivalence bar:
the broker adds latency of its own under this load. The more telling result is what happens when the predictor leaves
\sys{} behind. Running beside RabbitMQ as a sidecar that sets message priorities, it cut mean latency by \EThreeSidecar{},
well beyond what it achieved inside \sys{}. A plausible reason is that RabbitMQ's priorities never age, so short jobs stay
ahead through the whole flash sale, while \sys{}'s default aging eventually lifts waiting long jobs back to the top;
consistent with this, \sys{} with weights and pausing aging reached \EThreeWeighted{}. The experiment does not isolate aging,
so this is an explanation and not a measurement. It also shows the price of aging's absence: static priorities without
aging made the longest wait of a long job \EThreeStaticLongWait{} that of FIFO. In a separate cost measurement with
empty handlers, \sys{} delivered about \EFourHarbingerPeak{} messages per second at its limit against \EFourRabbitPeak{} for RabbitMQ and
\EFourRedisPeak{} for Redis, so it is the slowest of the three, which matters little against handlers that take milliseconds to
seconds but does show up in the FIFO gap above.

The benefit shrinks when backlogs last longer. In the thirty-minute run, with the broker on its own host, the reduction in
mean latency fell to \ETwoMeanA{} and \ETwoMeanB{} in the two repetitions, short of the pre-registered bar, although P99 and
the longest wait of a long job stayed level with FIFO and the predictor still beat the hand-tuned map. Outside overload the
predictor still cut latency substantially; it was the six-minute flash sale, which pushed FIFO's mean latency above a minute,
and the long recovery after it, that dominated the overall mean. At those waits the default aging promotes nearly every message to the
top level, so the order barely changes. This is the pattern the traces make explicit.

The simulations show it at its clearest (Table~\ref{tab:traces}). On the two serverless traces, predictive routing as built
beat FIFO by the pre-registered margin only once, at Huawei with load 0.8, where \HuaweiAEight{} is right at the
threshold. Everywhere else the strict-priority designs with the default aging gained little: on Azure not even the oracle moved mean
latency further than \AzureOracleNinetyFive{} from FIFO's, and on Huawei the oracle reached \HuaweiOracleEight{} at load 0.8 and the
predictor \HuaweiAEight{}. The backlogs are long enough for aging to erase most of the order. With weights and pausing aging the same
predictor passed five of the six cells, with reductions of \AzureBEight{} on Azure and \HuaweiBEight{} on Huawei at load 0.8 and
\AzureBNinetyFive{} and \HuaweiBNinetyFive{} at 0.95. The cost fell where theory says it should: on Huawei at load 0.8 the
median short job waited milliseconds instead of seconds, while long jobs waited a little longer and the all-message P99
stayed within \HuaweiBPNinetyNineEight{} of FIFO. The only failure, at Huawei with load 0.5, is a regime without queueing, in
which every policy is already at the service time. That the same weighted design works on two serverless providers, and
that the improvement from adding it over today's aging is \HuaweiBVsAEight{} on Huawei and \AzureBVsAEight{} on Azure at load 0.8,
suggests the aging mechanism, and not the particular predictor, is the lever under sustained overload.

What makes the serverless traces work is that their keys recur and are predictable. On Azure a per-key median predicts a
held-out duration to within a typical factor of \AzurePerKeyErrFactor{}, against \AzureGlobalErrFactor{} for a single global
median (Table~\ref{tab:predictability}); the keys that carry most jobs are the same functions over and over. The two HPC logs
are the opposite case, and the results there are the clearest failures in the paper. On LPC-EGEE only \LPCPredictedShare{}
of the \LPCJobs{} evaluated jobs got a prediction at load 0.8: \LPCColdShare{} belonged to keys that were too new and
\LPCStaleShare{} to keys whose history had expired. Of those stale-key jobs, \LPCStaleBacklogShare{} had themselves waited
more than a minute, which suggests that many keys were not idle so much as stuck behind a backlog that delivered no fresh
completions. Most jobs therefore took the default level, and the weighted design stayed close to FIFO, with mean latency
between \LPCBHalf{} and \LPCBNinetyFive{} of FIFO's and intervals that included zero. On DAS2-fs2 the keys were predictable enough to act on, and acting on them hurt. At load 0.8 the design with
weights and pausing aging raised mean latency by \DASBEight{}: short jobs gained several-fold, but long jobs carry most of
the work, and they waited several times longer, so that the guardrails failed with the all-message P99 at
\DASBPNinetyNineEight{} FIFO's and an upper bound of \DASBPNinetyNineUpperEight{}. The oracle with the same scheduler improved every
window there, with a mean reduction of \DASOracleBEight{}. The scheduler is therefore not the problem. The more likely culprit is the predictor's mistakes
on a heavy-tailed log, where a long job misjudged as short does great damage, though we have not isolated this.

Two further results are negative. We tested whether size-binned keys, which split a job type by the size of its input, would
improve prediction under load. They did improve the predictor's accuracy, but mean latency fell by only \SizeBinsMeanCI{}
against job-type keys, which missed the pre-registered bar, and for one job type whose cost does not follow its size it got
\SizeBinsInvoice{} worse. And before settling on statistics we compared eight online learners, including River models,
against per-key statistics and simple baselines offline. None of them passed both quality criteria on every workload, with
the strict rule that a model be no worse than both baselines everywhere and no tolerance for noise. As Section~\ref{sec:limits}
says, the workload gave a model little to find, so this is evidence that statistics suffice here, not that learning cannot help.
