\begin{abstract}
Message queues deliver work first-come-first-served unless an operator assigns priorities by hand, which requires knowing each
job type's cost and goes stale when costs change. We present \sys{}, a gRPC broker that learns each message key's expected
processing time from the durations it has already observed and routes the message into a multi-level queue, so that short
expected work is served first without per-endpoint priority configuration. The approach lowers mean and short-job latency; it does
not improve all-message tail latency, and it has failure regimes that we report. We evaluate it in experiments whose configurations and pass
criteria were frozen before the registered runs: a synthetic production-style job queue (nine job types, five seeds) on a
laptop and on AWS, the same workload against RabbitMQ and Redis, the Azure Functions and Huawei Cloud traces replayed through the real broker on AWS, and
two research-cluster logs simulated. On AWS, predictive routing cut mean latency by \EOneMeanVsFifoAbs{} against FIFO, with all-message P99 at \EOnePNinetyNine{}
of FIFO's, and it beat a hand-tuned static priority map by \EOneVsTunedAbs{}; run as a sidecar that sets RabbitMQ priorities, the same predictor cut RabbitMQ's mean latency by \EThreeSidecarAbs{}.
The gain is not free: the median latency of mid-cost jobs rose by \PerClassMidMin{} to \PerClassMidMax{}. What limits it under backlogs is the broker's default aging.
Predictive routing as built failed its gate in all \ReplayCells{} replayed trace cells; with aging switched off it cut the production flow's mean latency by a further \BaseNoAgingVsPredAbs{} and passed on Huawei Cloud at both loads tried.
An opt-in design with worker-time weights and pausing aging passed at loads 0.8 and 0.95 on both traces, cutting mean latency by \ReplayHuaweiBEightAbs{} to \ReplayHuaweiBNinetyFiveAbs{}
on Huawei and \ReplayAzureBEightAbs{} to \ReplayAzureBNinetyFiveAbs{} on Azure, and failed at load 0.5. The predictor is also not the only route to the gain: on the production flow a queue per job type with no prediction
had a mean latency \BasePtfVsPredAbs{} below predictive routing's, at the cost of a worse all-message P99, and on Huawei a static map built from the same history matched or beat the live predictor. On logs whose keys rarely recur
the predictor has little to learn from, and on one research-cluster log the weighted design raised mean latency (an interval that includes zero). The trace simulator first overstated the production flow's gain by about \SimValOverstatePct{}
but matched the broker to about a point on both replayed traces. All evidence is synthetic or trace-driven and from one cloud region, and the replayed handlers sleep; we claim no tail-latency improvement and no production readiness.
\end{abstract}
